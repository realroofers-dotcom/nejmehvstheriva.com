Nejmeh v. TherivaFilingsChronology

FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE · October 22, 2026

Michael Dell wants 25 million children to own stock. Why did a fund linked to him pay $3 million for 2 billion votes to help a small drug company multiply its shares?

Stockholders who owned Theriva shares then have since lost more than 99% of their value.

Mark Joseph Nejmeh, an SEC-registered large trader (Large Trader ID 71743954), is fighting for shareholders and scientists. His lawsuit, filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Nevada, Nejmeh v. Theriva Biologics, Inc., No. 3:26-cv-00705-ART-CSD, questions the financing of Theriva Biologics (NYSE American: TOVX), a Nevada corporation based in Rockville, Maryland, beside the Johns Hopkins University Montgomery County Campus in the I-270 biotech corridor. Among the trades in question are those of MSD Credit Opportunity Master Fund, L.P. ("MSD"), a fund that reports its holdings together with Michael S. Dell and his family office, MSD Capital.

The science. Theriva's own filings show:

  • VCN-01 for pancreatic cancer: the FDA granted Fast Track designation in May 2024, and the company reported results of its Phase 2b VIRAGE trial in metastatic pancreatic cancer in May 2025;
  • VCN-01 for retinoblastoma, a childhood eye cancer: Orphan Drug designation from the FDA and the European Medicines Agency and Rare Pediatric Disease designation from the FDA, which could make the company eligible for a Priority Review Voucher if the drug is approved by September 30, 2029. Vouchers of this kind sell for large sums: Denali Therapeutics sold one for $195 million in June 2026. At its October 20, 2026 closing price of about $0.21, Theriva's 45.9 million shares were worth about $9.6 million in total. Retinoblastoma is the cancer the investor Michael Burry, of "The Big Short," survived as a small child, losing an eye;
  • C. diff: U.S. patents issued in December 2024 and April 2025 covering SYN-004 (ribaxamase), its drug candidate designed to protect the gut microbiome and prevent C. difficile infection; its own list of press releases shows none with "patent" in the title since May 2020.

On September 30, 2025, Theriva cut about 32% of its workforce.

The transactions in question. On May 7, 2025, the day it reported its Phase 2b pancreatic cancer results, Theriva priced a public offering of about $7.5 million with a warrant for every share. It has now called another stockholder vote, on 500 million shares and 16.2 million warrant shares, for November 18, in person only, in Spain: 13 days before its answer to the lawsuit is due. Its proxy lists "reverse mergers" among the uses for the new shares, and says the board may do a reverse split "without stockholder approval." Its December 2025 prospectus says its preferred stock "could operate as a 'poison pill.'" Theriva has admitted that its 2026 proxy statement "contained errors."

So why can't a company with this science raise money on fair terms? Mr. Nejmeh believes the answer is simple: the games its management plays with financings and votes, and its poison pill.

The Dell link. In December 2025, Michael and Susan Dell pledged $6.25 billion to seed index-fund accounts for American children, saying they want every child to "see a future worth saving for." MSD's history with Theriva runs from 2017 to 2022. In 2017 it bought $12 million of Theriva preferred stock; in January 2021 Theriva cut that stock's conversion price from $18.90 to $1.50 per share and removed its redemption on a change of control. In July 2022, MSD paid Theriva about $3 million for new preferred stock carrying 2,000,000,000 votes. Theriva's own proxy statement gave the purpose: "to assist with procuring the vote necessary" to raise its authorized shares from 20 million to 350 million.

The others in the complaint. The lawsuit also discusses:

  • the funds holding Theriva's October 2025 warrants, as Theriva's SEC filings list them: Armistice Capital, funds managed by Empery Asset Management, Hudson Bay Master Fund, Anson funds, CVI Investments and Intracoastal Capital;
  • Theriva's banker on its financings, A.G.P./Alliance Global Partners;
  • a Schedule 13G filed on November 10, 2025, the day Theriva mailed the proxy for the first warrant vote, by a first-time filer, the Jeffrey Matthew Gryga Irrevocable Trust, reporting 739,211 shares as "7.7%" of the company, the percentage those shares were of Theriva's old share count. The complaint asks about this filing, and Mr. Nejmeh has said he will seek sworn testimony about it.

Mr. Nejmeh has asked the Court to take a close look at how these votes were won and, on October 22, to postpone the November 18 meeting until after Theriva answers the complaint. He contends that the way Theriva has been financed over the past five years has served the financiers at the expense of its stockholders.

Investors are told to avoid small companies. Mr. Nejmeh says it isn't the companies; it's the games played by the people who finance them. He contends that Theriva's management has shown a pattern of questionable decisions: financings and reverse splits that wiped out stockholders, and a poison pill, at a company with good science and no money to move it forward. Theriva never took its C. diff drug into a Phase 3 trial; instead it used money raised from stockholders to buy VCN Biosciences, a Spanish pancreatic cancer company, in 2022. The company has changed its name four times since 2006, from Sheffield Pharmaceuticals to Pipex, Adeona, Synthetic Biologics and now Theriva. Its chief executive's contract pays him two times his salary plus his target bonus if the company changes control, and it counts a reverse merger as a change of control. "I believe Theriva has never taken a product to the finish line," Mr. Nejmeh said, "while its management stays positioned to gain from a change of control."

"I'm doing this for the shareholders, yes, but more for the scientists trying to save lives. Their work is used as a carrot to attract investors, and then they are left to deal with financiers who don't share their goal of curing disease. Here is a company with a C. diff patent, a Fast Track drug for pancreatic cancer and an orphan drug for a childhood cancer, and I believe it cannot get fair funding while a poison pill and a possible reverse merger stand between its shareholders and its future. If there is nothing in the science, let it be. But if there is something in the science, fund it fairly." - Mark Nejmeh, plaintiff
